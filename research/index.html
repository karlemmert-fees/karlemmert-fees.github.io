research

What do prevention policies achieve for population health, for whom, and at what cost?

Prevention policies, such as taxes on sugary drinks, school food standards or programmes to prevent type 2 diabetes, are decided today. Most of their health effects, however, appear only years or decades later, and they rarely reach all social groups equally. My research asks what such policies achieve for population health, for whom, and at what cost. I focus on diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease in Germany and Europe.

To answer these questions I combine three perspectives: simulation models that project long-term effects before a policy is implemented, social epidemiology that describes who is at risk and why, and health economics that weighs health benefits against costs and asks how both are distributed.

Population health modeling

I develop and apply dynamic microsimulation models to evaluate policies and interventions before they are implemented. The models connect changing demographics, social factors, and behavioural and biological risk factors to long-term health outcomes, healthcare costs, and health inequalities.

Social epidemiology

I describe and explain health inequalities by their socioeconomic drivers, looking both at exposures (such as diet and smoking) and at outcomes such as incidence and mortality. Methods range from descriptive observational studies to quasi-experimental designs and simulation models.

Public health economics

I estimate the economic burden of NCDs and ask which policies can avoid direct and indirect healthcare costs. This includes cost-effectiveness and distributional cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA, DCEA), equity considerations, and econometric analyses of health insurance claims data.

PLOS Medicine · 2023

What a tax on sugary drinks could achieve in Germany

Germany has no tax on sugar-sweetened beverages. I developed IMPACT NCD Germany, a microsimulation of type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and stroke, to project the health and economic impact of tax designs used in other countries on German adults from 2023 to 2043. I cross-validated the results with an independently developed model.

Key contributions

  • A 20% tax could gain 106,000 QALYs and save €9.6 billion in societal costs over 20 years; a tiered tax like the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy could gain 192,300 QALYs and save €16.0 billion.
  • Tax designs that push manufacturers to reduce sugar content could achieve more than taxes that work only through higher prices.
  • Two independent models (a microsimulation and a multi-state life table model) produced similar results.

Diabetes Care · 2023

Is integrated care for diabetes and depression worth it in India?

Alongside the INDEPENDENT randomized trial at four urban clinics in India, I led the cost-effectiveness analysis of a collaborative care model with non-physician care coordinators, electronic registers and specialist-supported case review for 404 adults with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes and depression.

Key contributions

  • Collaborative care cost INR 309,558 (Int'l-$15,344) per QALY gained, just below a willingness-to-pay threshold of about three times GDP per capita.
  • Integrating collaborative care into routine clinical workflows would roughly halve the additional costs and raise the probability of cost-effectiveness to 89%.
  • The results support scaling up integrated mental and physical care in low- and middle-income countries, while scalability depends on local context.

European Journal of Public Health · 2022 · RIGOR award 2023

Combining quasi-experimental methods and simulation models

An integrative review, informed by international workshops of the EU Policy Evaluation Network, of how quasi-experimental methods (observed, short-term effects) and simulation models (projected, long-term effects) evaluate nutrition and physical activity policies, and how they can work together.

Key contributions

  • Sets out the assumptions behind instrumental variable, difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity designs, and the robustness and falsification checks needed to test them.
  • Summarizes the assumptions, strengths and limits of comparative risk assessment, Markov and microsimulation models.
  • Proposes an evidence cycle in which both approaches inform each other, from policy design to evaluation.

Advances in Nutrition · 2021

Mapping the models used to evaluate diet policies

A systematic scoping review of 56 economic evaluations, covering 136 model applications in 21 countries, that used simulation models to assess population-based dietary policies such as reformulation, taxation and labelling.

Key contributions

  • Reformulation (34 of 136 applications), taxation (27) and labelling (20) were the policies evaluated most often; salt, sugary drinks and fruit and vegetables were the most common targets.
  • 103 of 136 policy evaluations found the policy to be cost-saving.
  • Only 19 of 56 studies reported model validation and only 14 analysed equity impacts. Transparent validation and distributional analyses are needed.
